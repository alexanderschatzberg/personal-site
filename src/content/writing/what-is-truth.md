---
title: What is truth?
subtitle: A response to Truth.
date: 2026-10-01
---

This is a concept that gets thrown around a lot, but I have yet to come across a satisfying definition. Here's my attempt to write my way into a deeper understanding of what I believe truth to be.

In order to understand the world beyond our personal experience, we (conscious beings) rely on the physical remnants of past events. Some examples might include an oral story, a painting, a book, an Instagram reel, a hoof print, or a dinosaur fossil. Let's call these 'media'.

The truthiness of a piece of media refers to the maximum possible accuracy with which the original event(s) can be reconstructed from that media.

This means that the medium of your media matters a lot.

Different media elicit responses from different brain regions, which may be more or less similar to the theoretical stimulus triggered by the event(s) that the media represents. For the sake of example, let's say that a bar fight breaks out between two men after one spilled the other's beer. A drunk eyewitness testimony likening the brawl to a WWE match, complete with the chairs and suplexes, contains a great deal less truth than the CCTV camera showing the two men sluggishly rolling around on the ground. Cameras and microphones are super neat in their ability to trigger a very similar pattern of action potentials in the visual and audio cortices as the original experience did.

It also means that truth is not subjective, but that its accessibility varies greatly by individual.

A blind man may not be able to access the complete truth encoded in the video, as he must rely on a written description of the video, which may encode much of the important information (there were no folding chairs involved, unfortunately), but exclude other information (shirt colors, beer types, etc).

A chess grandmaster may be able to back out quite a bit of information about the mental states of the players from watching just a computerized representation of the board, particularly if he is close friends with each of them and they have all competed extensively together.

A chemist equipped with a mass spectrometer may be able to make all kinds of discoveries about the climate of bygone eras from looking at a piece of ice from a few thousand years ago.

Consider two people reading a tweet from an imaginary world leader about bombing the imaginary country of Eran. One has context around a particularly embarrassing/illegal/reprehensible scandal the leader may or may not have been involved in (he was) and knows about the funding that he's received from extremely wealthy [parties](https://www.timesofisrael.com/miriam-adelson-gives-100-million-to-trump-campaign-making-good-on-reported-pledge/) representing a nation that Eran would like to destroy, and the other does not. They will likely come to different conclusions about the leader's intentions behind bombing the country, which will be more or less accurate based on their priors.

There is truth in each of these signals, but it is sometimes tricky to back out. There are many factors, such as [cognitive biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cognitive_biases), education, intelligence, and experience, that may play a role in an individual's ability to map a piece of media back to the original event(s).

This also provides a clean definition of science as the pursuit of truth.
