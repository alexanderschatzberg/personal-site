"Good heavens, what insect can suck it?" - Charles Darwin

This orchid, Angraecum Sesquipedale, is native to Madagascar. James Bateman, who I've never heard, sent Darwin a sample of the unusual flower in 1854. He (Darwin, although presumably Bateman as well) was curious why an insect would be incentivized to pollinate this flower, as the nectar lies at the bottom of a foot long tube. He hypothesized that there was an insect with a ridiculously long proboscis that would allow it to drink the nectar while rubbing its hairy little body all over the plants' reproductive organs.

Darwin died before his theory could be verified, but, in 1903, the Xanthopan was discovered.

<img src="/images/writing/xanthopan.png" width="332" alt="Xanthopan moth with its long proboscis" />

Baby got proboscis.

Anyway. That's a pretty delicate little straw that the moth is entirely dependent on to feed itself, and the tube the flower stores the nectar in is also fairly fragile. There are far safer and more successful solutions to the plant sex problem.

This neat little pair of organisms prompts several thoughts:

1. Evolution is a statistical property of reproducing systems. If a mutation makes an organism marginally better at reproducing than its fellows, then its genes have a higher probability of being passed down, and its ancestors are more likely to be numerous. Mutations are uncoordinated and usually change few things at a time, which leads species to get stuck in local maxima. See [The Emergence of Life](/writing/the-emergence-of-life/) for more of my inconsequential thoughts on the matter. Thinking about this makes me pretty stoked for when we can design organisms to perform specific ecological functions, and I don't think we're too far away from that.
2. It's pretty romantic how these buggers evolved together and are such a perfect fit for each other.
3. Nature is delicate, and we should really pay attention to the species that we drive to extinction through our expansion and negligence.
