In the park over Green Key, you asked if I thought life was purely the result of stochastic processes. Given the state of our brains and the rate at which conversation was flowing, it's unsurprising that I had a hard time articulating my view in the few beats where it was relevant. Thinking over that conversation about a week later, I decided to write down my thoughts on this process (slightly) more coherently.

## Evolution as a Statistical Property

**The story I tell myself about how life emerged on our planet.**

Assume that the universe exists, and it's filled with matter governed by the laws of physics. Physics may be deterministic or nondeterministic, but it must allow the matter to accumulate into complex structures. Maybe our species will figure out why this is the case eventually, maybe not. Either way, it's pretty neat that physics works in such a way that it allows for everything below to happen.[^1]

In the universe we inhabit, there was a bunch of hot, fairly uniformly distributed hydrogen. Gravity did its thing, and we had the whole star formation/annihilation cycle that led to the formation of increasingly heavy elements. There were probably other cosmic objects (@gravitational_wave_collective) that formed many of these dense atoms too. At some point a few billion years ago, our solar system formed in a nebulous stellar graveyard, full of these dense elements. Gravity continued to do its thing, and the system stabilized.[^2]

Our planet was the correct distance from our star for liquid water (which may have already been present when the planet was formed, deposited by asteroids, or formed in the extreme conditions of early Earth — probably a group effort) to exist. This provided a medium for all these fun heavy atoms to mix together and form molecules. Carbon became the star of the show, as it has 4 valence electrons and readily forms a bunch of bonds with other atoms. As you let this stew mix for a long time, increasingly complex, increasingly unlikely permutations on those carbon-based molecules form. Let's call these "buggers". As time goes on, the probability that one bugger will be capable of reproducing asymptotically approaches 1. Once that happens, it will replicate and become more common. Call this dude and its copies `b`.

`b` will undergo a great number of mutations, most of which will just kill the individual who experiences them. As time goes on, however, the probability that one of these mutations leads the individual that experiences it (`b'`) to reproduce more readily than `b` approaches 1. `b'` will then become more prominent than `b`, simply because it's better at reproducing. This cycle continues for `b''`, `b'''`, etc., creating a directed acyclic graph of buggers with `b` at the root. Complex individuals emerge in this ocean because increased complexity allows for a wider range of features that may increase an organism's probability of reproducing, not because complexity itself is valuable or meaningful. This process gave rise to all the fun features, from the cones in our eyes to the neurons in our brains, that we get to enjoy as humans today.

So, yes, I think life emerged purely as the result of a stochastic process.

## What if life was seeded on Earth?

It's possible that some of those asteroids that hit our planet and deposited water also seeded the initial buggers. That, however, just kicks the above process to some other solar system.

[^1]: If you assume that there are an infinite (or very large) number of other universes, in each of which the (presumably) finite number of constants that govern physics are redrawn from certain distributions, then it isn't surprising that the constants are friendly in this universe. If they weren't, then life wouldn't have emerged and we wouldn't be pondering this question. Makes you wonder if there are a bunch of dead universes with a negative G.

[^2]: I also like to think of this process as a statistical property. If something has a high probability of ejecting/crashing into another body and you run the system for a long time, things that are likely to crash/eject will do so and you'll be left with a system where each body has a low probability of crashing/ejecting (aka "stable"). Not an earth-shattering idea (get it?), but it ties in with where I'm going with evolution.
