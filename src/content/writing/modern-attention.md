Take a second to think about attention. To define it.

You likely produced an intuitive, ethereal definition of attention, similar to William James’ 1890 interpretation. His definition, in possibly the most quoted line in all of attention psychology, claims that “[e]veryone knows what attention is. It is taking possession of the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seems several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought.” Over the last century and change, countless psychologists, philosophers, and neuroscientists have built upon this definition, developing countless frameworks to understand better how ‘attention’ operates across levels of neurological abstraction. While these mountains of research are incredibly useful to philosophers and neuroscientists studying attention, they tend to be overly complex for casual application. James’ definition remains broadly useful in everyday life.

So, why should we care about attention? It's hard to overstate how vital this resource is to humanity. From social interactions to learning new skills, few aspects of our lives escape its reach. Unfortunately, in the digital world, attention has become a battleground where its value is relentlessly exploited to the detriment of billions, a personal cognitive resource transformed into the primary commodity of some of the largest and most insidious corporations in history.

An attention market is essentially a system where our limited, valuable cognitive capacity - our attention - is traded as a commodity. In this market, companies don’t sell products or services directly to you; instead, they capture your attention and then monetize it, usually through advertising. This model is built on the premise that our attention is scarce and, once diverted, can be used to shape various patterns of behavior (consumption, voting, etc.). For instance, consider social media platforms like Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter. These platforms are designed to maximize the time you spend scrolling, liking, and sharing, all so they can serve targeted ads. Other examples are video-sharing platforms (e.g., YouTube, TikTok, etc.) or streaming services that use algorithmically curated content and clever tricks to keep you watching longer. Even search engines, by presenting you with sponsored results alongside your query responses, are participants in the attention market.

Attention markets present profound risks, as Watzl and Browne, professors of philosophy at the University of Oslo, argue. When we commodify attention, individuals trade not just a fleeting resource but a critical capacity, the very gateway to thought, memory, and decision-making, for services they need. In doing so, they risk entering a “low-control attention regime” where external forces gain the ability to shape what we perceive, think, and feel (Watzl and Browne). According to Watzl and Browne, the architecture of attention markets is designed to capture and direct our focus through strategically engineered environments. This design often significantly erodes our autonomous control, akin to what they describe in their “Den of Distraction” allegory. In this scenario, individuals may voluntarily enter a space where, despite the absence of physical restraints, they gradually forfeit their capacity for reflective thought due to relentless, manipulative distractions. The inherent risk of these markets is not confined to temporary lapses in attention; it potentially restructures how we engage with information and make decisions.

Attention markets carry some heavy negative externalities, but they're far from the only dangerous constructs that we interact with on a daily basis. Free markets nearly always carry a degree of intrinsic risk for participants: people will be free to make terrible, dangerous, and/or uncomfortable decisions.

Many office jobs, particularly those that require one to perform mind-numbing tasks in a drab, fluorescently lit office surrounded by people you hate, have an abundance of negative ramifications for a person's mental and physical health. The same goes for the consumption of alcohol, the purchase of mountaineering gear, or renting a car. Giving people the freedom to participate in these markets also gives them the power to misuse and abuse that freedom, potentially harming themselves and others. We've shown that restricting these markets in sufficiently harmful cases (e.g., hard drugs, slavery) can be effective in improving the overall quality of human life, but over-regulation often opens the door to a wide range of negative consequences (see Communist China, the Soviet Union, modern Russia, Venezuela, Germany during the 1930s, North Korea, and others). Freedom of information is often among the first things to go when nations begin their descent into authoritarianism, a bloody fall with terrible consequences for human rights and general quality of life. Restricting the type of media that a person may consume is a dangerous game, for installing legislation to restrict access to media sets a precedent. While banning certain forms of media (certain flavors of short-form content and pornography, for example) may have positive social consequences in isolation, the legislation used to ban that content can easily, cancerously spread to democratically vital sources of attention. Thus, it seems more logical to group the attention merchants' products with cars and firearms than with recreational heroin and slaves: dangerous, legal, and incredibly useful.

So, if attention is vital to democracy, and we can't just make distracting media illegal, how do we push back against the attention merchants?

Individuals must take responsibility for their habits. As beneficiaries of a free society, we are each responsible for our actions. If legislators are interested in guiding large-scale attention-based habits in a logical and universally beneficial direction, education is probably the most promising solution. Let's again consider the dangerous-yet-useful parallel between cars and social media. Governments around the world have recognized the dangers cars pose if operated by inexperienced drivers, so we have a lengthy, somewhat tedious set of hoops that all citizens must jump through if they want to get behind the wheel. Unfortunately, no such process exists for preparing children for the double-edged sword of the internet, and the potential harms of habitually scrolling Instagram for hours on end are far more subtle than those associated with drunkenly flooring it on a highway.

That said, education may not offer an escape from the web in which we are trapped. Humans are notoriously terrible at regulating themselves, demonstrated by the patterns of addiction that span our collective history and countless cultures.

I fear that the consequences of distraction addiction are not harsh enough to repel the majority of the population. Most addictive behaviors - smoking, hard drugs, gambling - have apparent enough consequences to disincentivize would-be users. Distraction addiction doesn't give you cancer or directly drain your bank account. Its tolls are far more subtle, leeching away at your life and capacity for sustained reflective thought. These, in my view, are two of the most vital components of a 'good' life, and it breaks my heart to think of the faceless billions gradually degrading themselves and their tragically short lives.
